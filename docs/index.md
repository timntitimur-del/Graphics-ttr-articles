# Tatar's Arabic alphabet

[Татарча](/tt/) | [English](/) | [Русский](/ru/) 

Since the adoption of Islam by Volga Bulgaria (İdel buye Bolğarı) in 922, the Tatar language has been written using the Arabic script.
The Arabic script officially remained in use as the writing system for Tatars until 1927, and continued to be used non-officially after. Under a 2012 [law](https://t.me/learntat/58), it is currently permitted in the Republic of Tatarstan to submit appeals to local self-government bodies in three writing systems, including the Arabic script.
All the information presented here is based on personal research, the analysis of texts written in the Tatar Arabic script found online, a study of the Arabic alphabet, conversations with native Arabic speakers, and inquiries made to my teacher, who studied the Arabic script at university. Those articles from Wikipedia, that I use as a links actually contains some mistakes (personally I found few), so be sceptical with them. 
# İske imlä
From Tatar: Old script.
In some sources, this is the name given to all Arabica from the X century to 1920, but we will only talk about [the reform of 1870](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%B0ske_iml%C3%A2_alphabet ).
## Writing rules
### Vowel graphemes
Three graphemes: alif (ا), ya (ي), and waw (و) - are used to represent the back "hard" sounds (A, O, I, U) and the sound İ. Moreover, these graphemes can represent absolutely any sound; for instance, the grapheme و can stand for either the sound *U* or *I*  - such as in the word *BULIP* (بولوب) or the *Э* sound in the word *TİYEŞ* (تيوش). Some back vowels in the middle of words are skipped; for example, in the word *XOQUQ* (حقوق), the 'o' is not written. The *alif* at the beginning of a word is also used to indicate that the word begins with a vowel. For example, اوز is read as *ÜZ*, and وز would be read as *WÖZ*, *WEZ*, etc. And *AWIZ* (i.e., the Tatar word for "mouth") is written as آوز with an "elongated" or "doubled" *alif* (the first to indicate that the word begins with a vowel, and the second to show that it is an *A*). If the word began with *I*, only a single *alif* was written, for example, *ITTIFАQ* (اتفاق) (Later in this article in the*Yaña imlä* section, I will explain why it was so). Front vowels are written only at the end or in the middle. To understand exactly how a particular word is pronounced, one must read it with vocalization marks, i.e. with the "dashes" or "hooks" placed above or below the grapheme. In most cases, you just have to memorize the correct spelling (though I could be wrong).

# Yaña imlä
From Tatar: New script.
Yaña imlä emerged in 1920 as a result of a reform of Иске имлә in USSR. It was officially used between 1920 and 1927. Later, in the early 21st century, it would be taught in schools for a short time. The fact is, it turns out that the spelling in Яңа имлә varied slightly over time. For example, I studied it using [this](https://miras.info/projects/mirasxane/books/715-azhdaa-zur-sugysh-.html) edition Acdaha. Zur Suğış. 1921, 6th State Printing House, Kazan. However, there are other preserved written sources where the rules are slightly different, for example [this](https://miras.info/projects/mirasxane/books/585-atlarny-jahshyrtu-.html ) 1927 edition. There are also pages from 2003 textbooks.
![Pages from student's books](/images/book.jpg)
Let's sort out the rules of writing. For the sake of clarity, I will refer to the version used in the 1921 edition as the "early version," and the version used in the 1927 and 2003 editions as the "new version."
## Writing rules
### Vowel graphemes
Unlike İske imlä, Yaña one have a richer set of vowels.

Like iske imlä, a special mark must be placed to indicate that a word begins with a vowel sound. Actually it caused by the perso-arabic rule that every word must start with consonant. "ا" as "consonant" present $[ʔ]$ [glottal stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glottal_stop) sound in iske imlä and early yaña imlä. The same did "ئ". In my opinion, the earlier version was slightly more convenient because "ئ" was written before soft vowels, indicating that, according to the law of vowel harmony, the entire word would be pronounced with a soft sounds, and "ا" was used before hard vowels, indicating the opposite. In the new writing system, you are asked to determine softness of sounds based on the vowels indicated by the preceding letters. The letters preceding the word help determine whether it is hard, indicated by ق, ع, or ا (i.e., Ğ, Q, or A), or soft, indicated by ك, گ, or ە (i.e., G, K, or Ä). Thereby, in the early version, "village" is written as "آول" and "he" as "اول", whereas in the new version: "village" is "ئاول" and "he" is "ئول".

### Phonetic assimilation
When studying Tatar, you have likely encountered the problem of choosing plural endings. Do you say "planlar" instead of "plannar"? It's okay, you're still tatar. Why? So in yaña imlä, as actually also in iske imlä is phonetic assimilation. I.e. we say "plannat", but write "planlar". Read "n" instead of "l", because "n" - a nasal sound, so a nasal "n" is needed. Or we add “Kitap” -> “Kitabım”, but we write “Kitab -> Kitabım”, because assimilation. We read 'p' instead of 'b' because the "k" and "t" are voiceless, meaning the final syllable is also voiceless. It is similar to the Arabic word النور, for example: although spelled *al-nūr*, it is pronounced *an-nūr* due to phonetic assimilation.
